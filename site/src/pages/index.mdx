## How to predict the AUC

Follow five steps to predict the AUC in the target population. The AUC is the
area under the ROC curve: the chance that a random case scores above a random
control, where 0.5 is no discrimination at all.

<Predictor />

<div class="chain">
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 1 · calculate F<sub>ST</sub> between the source and the target population</div>

$F_{ST}$ measures how far two populations have diverged in allele frequency.
$t$ counts the generations since they separated, and $N_e$ is the effective
size of each population over that period.

$$
F_{ST} = \frac{t}{t + 2N_e}
$$

This $F_{ST}$ follows the Hudson convention. The Nei $G_{ST}$ of the same two
populations is a smaller number, by up to a factor of two. Steps 2 to 5 need
the Hudson value.

</div>
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 2 · calculate the additive variance the score explains in the target population</div>

$V_A$ is the additive genetic variance that the score explains in the source
population. The SNPs in a score, the single nucleotide polymorphisms it carries,
rarely include the causal variants. Each SNP tags a causal variant through
linkage disequilibrium instead, and $\rho^{2}$ is the fraction of that tagging
which survives in the target population.

You cannot read $\rho^{2}$ off a reference panel, because it depends on which
causal variants the score's SNPs tag and on how that tagging changes between the
two populations. You measure it instead: score one target cohort, measure the
accuracy ratio against the source population, and that ratio bounds $\rho^{2}$
from above once you divide out $1 - F_{ST}$. Wright-Fisher simulations of three
demographies measured values from 0.50 to 1.06, so treat a value near 1 as a
claim that needs its own measurement rather than a safe default.

$$
V_{\text{target}} = \rho^{2}\,(1 - F_{ST})\,V_A
$$

$F_{ST}$ and the lost LD tagging each reduce the additive variance the score
explains. Neither one predicts the other.

</div>
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 3 · calculate R² on the liability scale</div>

$R^{2}$ is the fraction of the liability variance that the score explains in
the target population. $V_E$ is the residual liability variance, which the
score does not explain.

$$
R^{2}_{\text{target}} = \frac{V_{\text{target}}}{(1 - F_{ST})V_A + V_E}
$$

The denominator is the target population's whole liability variance, and lost
LD tagging does not shrink it. Tagging loss attenuates the covariance between
the score and the phenotype. The genetic variance the score fails to capture
stays in the phenotype. Dividing by $V_{\text{target}} + V_E$ instead cancels $\rho^{2}$
out of the ratio and overstates how much accuracy transfers, by 15% to 112% in
Wright-Fisher simulation.

</div>
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 4 · calculate the liability threshold and the case and control moments</div>

$K$ is the trait prevalence in the target population, and $T$ is the liability
threshold that produces that prevalence. $i$ is the mean liability among cases
and $i_c$ is the mean liability among controls.

$$
T = \Phi^{-1}(1-K), \qquad i = \frac{\varphi(T)}{K}, \qquad i_c = -\frac{iK}{1-K}
$$

$v_1$ and $v_0$ are the score variances among cases and among controls, each
one **divided by $R^{2}$**: the score variance among cases is $R^{2}v_1$, not
$v_1$. Step 5 uses the divided form, so the $R^{2}$ cancels there.

$$
v_1 = 1 - R^{2}\,i\,(i - T), \qquad v_0 = 1 - R^{2}\,i_c\,(i_c - T)
$$

The score varies by a different amount among cases than among controls. Step 5
needs both variances.

</div>
<div class="step" data-key>
<div class="label">step 5 · calculate the AUC</div>

$$
\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{(i - i_c)\sqrt{R^{2}}}{\sqrt{v_1 + v_0}}\right)
$$

Wray et al. 2010 published this formula. It contains no fitted number.

We supplied a true $R^{2}$, inverted the formula for the prevalence, and
recovered 0.149. The true prevalence was 0.150.

A version of this formula that sets $v_1 = v_0$ reports an AUC 0.068 too low on
a binary trait.

</div>
</div>

## The effect-size error has three terms

A polygenic score carries one effect size per SNP. The score loses accuracy in
the target population when those effect sizes differ from the true effect sizes
in the target population. We proved that the $R^{2}$ lost equals the squared
effect-size error, weighted by the LD between SNPs in the target population.

That effect-size error has three terms, and each term falls with a different
kind of data.

**The source GWAS estimates each effect size with sampling error.** This term
falls in proportion to one over the GWAS sample size. A score that carries more
SNPs has more effect sizes to estimate, so it pays more of this term.

**The true effect sizes differ between the two populations,** through allele
frequency differences and through LD differences. Genotyped target cohorts
measure that effect-size difference and correct the score for it. This term
falls in proportion to one over the number of target cohorts. Each SNP needs
its own correction, so a score that carries more SNPs again pays more.

**A residual effect-size difference stays.** Target cohorts cannot estimate an
effect size for a SNP they do not genotype, or for a SNP whose minor allele
frequency is too low to measure. This term falls with no amount of data.

A larger source GWAS reduces the sampling-error term only. It does nothing to
the residual term. The next two rules apply to the between-population term,
which is the only term that target genotyping reduces.

<ErrorParts />

### How many target cohorts to collect

A target cohort is a group of people from the target population whom you
genotype and phenotype, so that you can re-estimate the effect sizes in that
population. Genotyping gives you a fixed total number of SNPs, and you choose
how to divide those SNPs between cohorts.

The LD decay length is the number of SNPs across which linkage disequilibrium
stays high. Two SNPs closer together than this length are correlated, so the
second one adds little information beyond the first.

$$
\text{best SNPs per cohort} \;\approx\; 3.3 \times \text{LD decay length}
$$

More separate cohorts reduce the between-population term, and that term falls
in proportion to one over the number of cohorts, because each cohort
re-estimates the effect sizes independently. A cohort that holds fewer SNPs
than about 3.3 LD decay lengths cannot estimate its own effect sizes, and past
that point more cohorts raise the error again.

The ratio stays between 3.27 and 3.50 across a factor of 1000 in the LD decay
length, so this rule holds for other SNP densities.

A population with longer-range linkage disequilibrium needs fewer SNPs for the
same accuracy, because each SNP tags more of the genome.

### How many times to fit each cohort

You fit the calibration of one cohort several times, each fit on a different
subsample of that cohort. The reliability is the predictable variance divided
by the predictable variance plus the estimation noise that survives the
averaging of those fits. It runs from 0 to 1.

$$
\text{fits per cohort} \;\geq\; \frac{\text{noise variance of one fit}}{\text{predictable variance}}\cdot\frac{\text{reliability}}{1-\text{reliability}}
$$

You do not need the two variances separately. **Fit one cohort a fixed number
of times, measure the reliability you reach, and the ratio follows:**

$$
\frac{\text{noise variance of one fit}}{\text{predictable variance}} \;=\; \text{fits run}\times\frac{1-\text{reliability reached}}{\text{reliability reached}}
$$

Sixteen fits that reach a reliability of 0.153 put that ratio near 88 for that
cohort. The condition holds in both directions, so it tells you when you have
enough fits and when you do not.

The cost grows fast as the reliability approaches 1. Each further nine of
reliability costs about ten times more fits. Sixteen fits per cohort reach a
reliability of 0.153, and a reliability of 0.8 needs about 350 fits per cohort.

## Two limits on the AUC

Two effects limit the AUC in the target population. A larger sample removes the
first one. No sample size removes the second one.

<div class="pair">
<div class="card">

### SNPs in perfect linkage disequilibrium

Two SNPs in perfect LD carry the same genotype information, and their LD
$r^{2}$ equals 1. That $r^{2}$ is the squared correlation between two
genotypes, and it is not the $R^{2}$ of steps 3 to 5, which is the fraction of
the liability the score explains. LD pruning drops one SNP from every such
pair.

**Pruning at $r^{2}=1$ is not sufficient.** The residual effect-size
difference appears once some joint
genotype cell of a SNP pair carries zero probability, and that can happen far
below $r^{2}=1$: in an exact sweep over two-locus couplings, 56 of 72
configurations carrying a vanishing haplotype lost coverage at $r^{2}<1$, the
worst at $r^{2}=1/81\approx0.012$. Complete linkage costs nothing at all unless
the two loci have equal allele frequencies, and at allele frequency $1/2$ it
costs nothing even then.

So no $r^{2}$ threshold certifies that the residual term is zero. The condition
needs a positive floor on every joint genotype cell.
<div class="caution">

**CAUTION: Always quote the sample size that a difference costs to measure.**
Two populations can differ in the mean of a standardized genotype, in its
variance, or only in a higher moment of it. Each further level of that moment
tower doubles the moment order, and the sample size needed to estimate a level
grows with the square of the scale of that level: of order $4\times10^{2}$
people at the third level, $9\times10^{4}$ at the fourth, and $5\times10^{9}$
at the fifth. Rare variants start higher up the tower. The squared scale of the
first level is about 49 at minor allele frequency 0.01, against 1 at minor
allele frequency 0.5.

A difference that sits above the third level is a hard limit in a real study. A
larger sample removes it in principle only.

</div>

Worse for practice, that condition asks for *positive* probability, and a
positive probability is not observable. At allele frequency $3/10$ with 500,000
samples the rarest genotype cell is missed 22% of the time at $r^{2}=0.98$ and
every time at $r^{2}\geq0.998$, where seeing it would need about
$7\times10^{7}$ samples. The sample size, not $r^{2}$, sets the threshold that
binds a real study.

And linkage is not even the dominant cause. At allele frequency $1/20$ with
$r^{2}=0$ exactly — perfect linkage *equilibrium* — 500,000 samples lose a cell
in 5% of runs. Raising $r^{2}$ to $0.81$ there makes the rarest cell *more*
common, so more LD helps. A rule phrased in $r^{2}$ gets both of these
backwards.

</div>
<div class="card">

### Environment that changes with ancestry

Environmental exposure often varies across cohorts along the same gradient as
ancestry does, and socioeconomic status is the usual example. Where the two
gradients are collinear, a one-parameter family of splits between the genetic
effect per unit of ancestry and the environmental effect per unit of exposure
produces exactly the same calibration shift in every cohort. The shifts are
equal, not close.

No number of cohorts separates the genetic effect from the environmental
effect. Sensitivity, positive predictive value, exceedance probability and quantile
comparisons
collapse together as well, so no threshold on the score separates the two
effects either.

<div class="caution">

**CAUTION: Collect two cohorts at the same nonzero $F_{ST}$ from the source
population and with different environmental exposures.** Those two cohorts
determine the split. Cohorts that differ only in $F_{ST}$ never determine it,
however many you collect.

</div>

The cohort rule above still holds here, and more cohorts still reduce the
between-population term. Those cohorts attribute none of that term to genetics
rather than to environment unless their environmental exposures differ at
matched $F_{ST}$.

</div>

## What these formulas omit

None of the three terms includes an environmental contribution. Where
environmental exposure varies with ancestry, that exposure enters the same
per-cohort calibration shift as drift does, and no formula on this page carries
it separately.

We proved the non-identifiability that the section on environment states above.
We did not measure how large the environmental contribution is, and nothing on
this page bounds it.

These formulas also omit admixture, assortative mating, ascertainment in the
source GWAS, and every allele frequency spectrum difference beyond $F_{ST}$.
