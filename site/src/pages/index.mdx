## How to predict the accuracy

Follow five steps to predict the AUC in the target population.

<Predictor />

<div class="chain">
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 1 · calculate the genetic distance</div>

$F_{ST}$ measures how far two populations have diverged in allele frequency.
$t$ counts the generations since they separated, and $N_e$ is the effective
size of each population over that period.

$$
F_{ST} = \frac{t}{t + 2N_e}
$$

This $F_{ST}$ follows the Hudson convention, which is the quantity a coalescent
simulation measures. The Nei $G_{ST}$ of the same two populations is a
different number, smaller by up to a factor of two. Steps 2 to 5 need the
Hudson value.

</div>
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 2 · calculate the surviving signal</div>

$V_A$ is the variance that the score explains in the source population. The
markers in a score rarely include the causal variants. They stand in for them
through LD instead, and $\rho^{2}$ is the fraction of that LD which survives in
the target population.

$$
V_{\text{target}} = \rho^{2}\,(1 - F_{ST})\,V_A
$$

Genetic distance and LD each remove signal. Neither number predicts the
other.

</div>
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 3 · calculate the accuracy</div>

$R^{2}$ is the fraction of the liability that the score explains. $V_E$ is the
variance that the score does not explain.

$$
R^{2}_{\text{target}} = \frac{V_{\text{target}}}{V_{\text{target}} + V_E}
$$

</div>
<div class="step">
<div class="label">step 4 · calculate four numbers from the prevalence</div>

$K$ is the trait prevalence. $T$ is the liability threshold that produces it.
$i$ and $i_c$ are the mean liabilities among cases and among controls. $v_1$
and $v_0$ are the score variances in those two groups.

$$
T = \Phi^{-1}(1-K), \qquad i = \frac{\varphi(T)}{K}, \qquad i_c = -\frac{iK}{1-K}
$$

$$
v_1 = 1 - R^{2}\,i\,(i - T), \qquad v_0 = 1 - R^{2}\,i_c\,(i_c - T)
$$

The two variances differ. You need both of them in step 5.

</div>
<div class="step" data-key>
<div class="label">step 5 · calculate the AUC</div>

$$
\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{(i - i_c)\sqrt{R^{2}}}{\sqrt{v_1 + v_0}}\right)
$$

Wray et al. 2010 published this formula. It contains no fitted number.

We supplied a true $R^{2}$ and solved for the prevalence. The answer was 0.149.
The true prevalence is 0.150.

A formula that treats the two variances as equal is 0.068 AUC too low on binary
traits.

</div>
</div>

## The error has three parts

Each part shrinks with a different kind of data.

$$
\mathrm{Risk} \;\propto\; \underbrace{\frac{\text{parameters}}{2 \times \text{people in the source GWAS}}}_{\text{a larger GWAS reduces this}}
$$

$$
+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2 \times \text{cohorts} \times \text{information per cohort}}}_{\text{more separate cohorts reduce this}}
\;+\; \underbrace{(\text{unremovable part})^{2}}_{\text{neither reduces this}}
$$

"Parameters" counts what the source model fits. "Information per cohort" is how
much one target cohort tells you about the transfer. The third part is the
error that stays whatever data you collect.

A larger source GWAS therefore reduces the first part only. It does nothing to
the third part.

### How many target cohorts to collect

$$
\text{best markers per cohort} \;\approx\; 3.3 \times \text{LD decay length}
$$

Take a fixed total number of genotyped markers. More separate target cohorts
reduce the error, and the error drops in proportion to one over the cohort
count. Each cohort must still contain enough markers for its own calibration.
Past that point more cohorts make the error worse.

The LD decay length is measured in markers. The ratio stays between 3.27 and
3.50 across a factor of 1000 in the LD decay length, so you can use this rule
in other studies.

A longer LD decay length also reduces the error at a fixed marker count.

<CohortCurve />

### How many times to fit each cohort

$$
\text{fits needed} \;=\; 88 \times \frac{\text{reliability}}{1 - \text{reliability}}
$$

A fit uses one subsample of the cohort. Reliability runs from 0 to 1. The
constant 88 depends on the cohort and on the trait, and one measurement fixes
it.

The condition is exact in both directions. You therefore know when you have
enough fits and when you do not.

The cost grows very fast as $\tau$ approaches 1. Each further nine costs
about ten times more fits.

Sixteen fits per cohort give a reliability of 0.153. Reliability 0.8 needs
about 350 fits per cohort.

## Two limits

Two separate effects limit the accuracy. You can pay for the first one with
more samples. No sample size removes the second one.

<div class="pair">
<div class="card">

### Markers in perfect linkage disequilibrium

Two markers in perfect LD carry the same information. Their LD $r^{2}$, which
is a different quantity from the accuracy $R^{2}$ above, equals 1. LD pruning
drops one marker from every such pair.

**After pruning, the unremovable part of the error is zero.** The whole error
then shrinks as the sample size and the cohort count grow.
<div class="caution">

**CAUTION: Always quote the cost with this result.** Some differences between
populations are cheap to measure and some are very expensive. A difference that
appears only indirectly needs a sample size that grows as a power of how
indirect it is. The exponent doubles with each step of indirectness, so the
count grows without limit.

An expensive difference is a hard limit in a real study. Enough samples remove
it in principle only.

</div>

We proved one direction: LD pruning removes the fixed error. We did not prove
the reverse direction. That proof needs a worked example, and nobody built one
yet.

</div>
<div class="card">

### Environment that changes with ancestry

Environment often varies along the same axis as ancestry. When it does, a whole
range of different genetic and environmental splits produces exactly the same
result in every cohort. The results are equal, not close.

No amount of cohort data separates the genetic part from the environmental
part. This is true for AUC, sensitivity, PPV, and every other threshold
measure.

<div class="caution">

**CAUTION: Collect two cohorts that have the same genetic distance and
different environments.** That pair separates the genetic part from the
environmental part. Cohorts that differ only in genetic distance never separate
them, whatever their number.

</div>

This result limits the rule on how many cohorts to collect. More cohorts
reduce the genetic-distance part of the error. They reduce this part only when the
environments differ.

</div>
</div>

## What these laws leave out

None of the three parts contains a term for environment. Socioeconomic
measures can explain as much difference between individuals as genetic distance
explains. The largest term is then outside every formula on this page.

We proved that this gap exists, and the section on environment that changes
with ancestry states it. The gap still restricts the value of the other
results.

These laws also leave out admixture, assortative mating, ascertainment in the
source GWAS, and any difference in allele frequency spectra beyond $F_{ST}$.
